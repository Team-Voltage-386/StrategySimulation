The SEARCH tab

Your strategy has numbers in it. How long to wait before giving up on a game piece, how far to look for one, how long to stay on a job before switching. Somebody picked those numbers by feel, probably for last year's robot. The SEARCH tab picks better ones for this robot, by playing thousands of matches and paying attention.

This guide assumes you have never heard of CMA-ES, evolution strategies, or optimisation. You do not need any of that. You do need about fifteen minutes and a willingness to be suspicious of good news.

01Why the numbers matter

Open any strategy in the STRATEGY tab and you will find rules: if I am holding nothing, go collect coral. Each rule carries numbers. How close a game piece has to be before it counts as worth fetching. How many seconds to commit to a job before you are allowed to change your mind. How long a condition has to stay true before you believe it.

Those numbers are not neutral. A robot that drives at 150 in/s wants different ones from a robot that drives at 90. A robot that takes four seconds to intake wants different ones from a robot that takes one. But the strategy files were written once, by a person, with one robot in mind — and then we use them to compare every robot design we are thinking about building.

That is the problem this tab exists to fix. When the SWEEP tab tells you a faster drivetrain scores 8 more points, what it really measured was "a faster drivetrain, driven by a strategy written for a slower one". The honest question is what each design can do at its best — and to answer that, each design needs its own numbers.

Where to find it

Run the simulator (run.bat) and look at the tabs along the top: MATCH, STRATEGY, SWEEP, SEARCH. This guide is about the last one.

02How the search actually works

The method is called CMA-ES. The name is not worth memorising. The idea behind it takes about a minute.

Imagine finding the deepest part of a lake

You cannot see the bottom. All you can do is drop a weighted line somewhere and read off a depth. So you do this:

  1. Drop a scattered handful of lines around your best guess — say a dozen of them, spread out in a rough circle.
  2. Read all twelve depths. Keep the deepest few. Ignore the rest.
  3. Move your guess toward the ones that came back deepest.
  4. Notice the shape of where the deep ones were. If they were all off to the north-east, stretch your next scatter that way. If they were tightly clustered, shrink it.
  5. Repeat.

That is the whole algorithm. Each round is a generation. Each dropped line is a candidate — a complete copy of your strategy with slightly different numbers in it. The depth reading is the average score that candidate got across a batch of simulated matches.

Step 4 is the clever part and the reason we use CMA-ES rather than just trying each number one at a time. Some of these numbers interact: a longer commitment time might only pay off if the search radius is also larger. Testing them one at a time never finds that. Watching the shape of the good region does.

What it is allowed to change — and what it is not

This matters, and it is the reason the output stays trustworthy. The search can turn the dials on your strategy. It cannot add a rule, delete a rule, reorder your priorities, or swap one tactic for another. The strategy that comes out has exactly the rules you wrote, in the order you wrote them, with different numbers.

So the result is a normal strategy file. You can open it in the STRATEGY tab, read it, disagree with it, and edit it by hand. There is no model to interrogate and nothing to take on faith.

03The trap that catches everyone

Here is the thing that will fool you, and it fooled this tool on its very first run. Read this section twice.

Two identical strategies do not score the same. Game pieces start in slightly different places, robots accelerate slightly differently, and the same strategy can win a match by 12 or lose it by 4 depending on nothing at all. That spread is large — often larger than the improvement you are hunting for.

Now think about what the search does. It tries hundreds of candidates and keeps the one with the highest score. If every candidate is judged on the same small handful of matches, then the winner is not necessarily the best strategy. It is partly just the one that got luckiest on those particular matches. Pick the maximum of a few hundred noisy numbers and you have picked mostly noise.

Measured, not hypothetical

The first real run of this tool searched 6 generations with 6 candidates each, judging every candidate on 4 matches. This is what it reported, and what was actually true:

What the search said +9.5 points, on the 4 matches it used to pick the winner
What survived on fresh matches +0.2 points, on 12 matches the search had never seen

98% of the improvement was not real. It was the winner fitting the exact starting positions of four particular matches. Nothing was broken; this is simply what taking a maximum over noisy measurements does.

The fix, and why it is switched on by default

At the end of every search, the tool re-runs two strategies — your original and the tuned winner — on a completely fresh set of matches that played no part in choosing the winner. Nothing was selected to look good on those, so nothing can fake it. That number is called the confirmed result, and it is the only one the RESULT tab shows you in large type.

Which leads to the single most useful piece of advice in this guide:

If your gains keep evaporating on confirmation, raise "Matches per candidate", not "Generations". More generations means looking harder at a measurement you cannot trust. More matches per candidate means trusting the measurement more. Only one of those helps.

04The other trap: one fixed opponent

Confirmation matches fix the noise problem. It does not fix a second, quieter problem: the Opponent dropdown a few numbers up. Every candidate the search tries is judged against whichever single strategy sits in that box — by default, full_defense.

That means the search does not really find a good strategy. It finds a good counter to that one opponent, and the score cannot tell you which of those two things just happened. Tune against full_defense and you may end up with numbers that fall apart against a strategy that ignores you entirely.

The fix: grade against a field, not one robot

Check "Grade against a hall of fame instead" and the Opponent box stops mattering. Every candidate is now judged against a field: every hand-written strategy that ships with the game, plus a sample drawn from an archive file of past winners. The field is re-drawn each generation, so the search cannot settle into beating one lucky sample of opponents either.

The archive is the point. Point two searches at the same .json file and the second one has to prove itself against the first one's winner, not just against six strategies a person wrote by hand. Run it enough times and the field gets genuinely hard to fool.

In exchange for that, it costs more: roughly archive sample size + hand-written count times as many matches per candidate as one fixed opponent does. The budget readout at the bottom of the setup panel already accounts for this once the box is checked.

Exploitability: the number this adds

Alongside the usual score, a hall-of-fame run reports a payoff matrix — how the winner did against each opponent in the field — and one summary number, exploitability: how much the single worst matchup in that field beat it by.

{{TAGS:hof}}

This particular run found no real gain either — worth keeping in the guide rather than swapping in a flattering number, for the same reason section 3 keeps its own null result. The lesson does not change: exploitability of 0 means the winner beat every opponent in this field, not that it is unbeatable. It only means nothing in this particular archive found a hole. A bigger archive, or a smarter human opponent at the next competition, might still find one.

05Walkthrough: setting up a run

The left-hand column asks three questions in the order you should answer them: what to tune, how hard to look, and what that will cost. The right-hand panel shows you exactly which numbers are on the table.

{{TAGS:setup}}

Sensible first settings

Leave everything at its default except the two that decide whether you get an answer or a ghost: keep Matches per candidate at 16 or higher, and leave Confirmation matches switched on. Then watch the budget readout before you press RUN — if it says hours and you have twenty minutes, turn Generations down rather than turning matches down.

06Walkthrough: watching it work

Once a run starts, the PROGRESS panel is where you find out whether it is still learning anything. The window stays usable throughout, and STOP is always available — it just waits for the round in flight to finish first.

{{TAGS:progress}}

Reading the curve

  • Bright line rising: still finding better numbers. Let it run.
  • Bright line flat for several generations: it has stopped learning. Extra generations from here are mostly wasted — and remember, a flat line is not a failure. It may simply mean your hand-written numbers were already good.
  • Dim line climbing toward the bright one: normal and healthy. It means the scatter of guesses is narrowing in around a promising region.
  • "step" in the log shrinking toward zero: the search is converging and will stop on its own, possibly before it uses all your generations. That is not a bug and you are not being short-changed.

07Walkthrough: reading the verdict

When the search finishes it switches to the RESULT panel. Everything about how this panel is laid out follows from section 3: the number you can trust is large, and the number that flatters itself is small and grey.

{{TAGS:result}}

The three verdicts

What it saysWhat it meansWhat to do
Real gain The tuned numbers genuinely beat the hand-written ones on matches nobody cherry-picked. Save it, open it in the STRATEGY tab, and read what changed. If a number looks absurd, that is worth understanding before you trust it.
No real gain The difference is smaller than the match-to-match noise. Your original numbers were already fine. A completely legitimate outcome, and useful information. If you suspect there is something to find, raise Matches per candidate and try once more.
Worse on fresh matches The tuned strategy actually lost ground. Almost always means the search was fitting noise hard. Raise Matches per candidate substantially. Do not save this result.

What "saved" means

Saving writes an ordinary strategy JSON file next to the others. Nothing downstream knows or cares that a search produced it — the STRATEGY tab will open it, the MATCH tab will run it, the SWEEP tab will sweep it. It is your strategy with different numbers, and you are free to overrule any of them.

09When something looks wrong

SymptomWhat is going on
RUN is greyed out The chosen strategy has no tunable numbers — everything in it is structural or switched off. The WHAT MOVES panel says so. Pick a different strategy.
Big gain during the search, nothing after confirmation The classic case from section 3. The search fitted the luck in its own matches. Raise Matches per candidate.
It stopped before using all its generations Deliberate. The scatter of guesses shrank to nothing, meaning the search converged. The log line says why.
A tuned number sits exactly on its limit The search wanted to go further and the range stopped it. The result is still valid, but the limit chose the answer, not the search. Worth mentioning to a mentor.
Everything is slow and the rest of the app stutters A search uses every CPU core it is given. Turn CPU workers down, or run it when nobody needs the machine.
The tuned strategy scores well but drives strangely Save it, then watch it in the MATCH tab. The search optimises score and nothing else; it has no opinion about whether the robot looks sensible doing it.
The Opponent dropdown is greyed out Normal, not broken. "Grade against a hall of fame instead" is checked, so every candidate faces a field instead of the single opponent in that box — see section 4.
Exploitability says 0 and it still lost at competition 0 means it beat everything in this field, not that it cannot lose. A larger archive sample, or a human opponent trying something the archive never did, can still find a hole. Read it as "unexploited so far", not "unbeatable".

10What it cannot do

Being clear about this saves you from expecting the wrong thing.

  • It cannot invent a strategy. It tunes the one you give it. If your rules are wrong, better numbers will not save them — that is a job for you and the STRATEGY tab.
  • It cannot add, remove, or reorder rules. Deliberate: that is what keeps the output readable. Searching over structure is a separate, much harder problem and it is not built yet.
  • It cannot switch on something you left off. A setting you left blank means "no limit", and treating a blank as a number would quietly add a constraint you never wrote.
  • It cannot tell you about a game piece the simulator models badly. It optimises against the simulation, so anything the simulation gets wrong, the search will happily exploit.
  • It does not know what a good match looks like. Only what a high score looks like.
  • The hall of fame cannot prove a strategy unbeatable. Zero exploitability means nothing in the current field found a hole — not that no hole exists. See section 4.

11Glossary

Candidate
One complete copy of your strategy with slightly different numbers in it. A generation tries several at once.
Generation
One round of the search: try a batch of candidates, score them all, move the guess toward the good ones.
CMA-ES
The method described in section 2. The letters stand for Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy, which is a precise way of saying "it learns the shape of the good region as it goes".
Seed
A number that fixes all the randomness in one match. Same seed, same match, every time. "Matches per candidate" is really "how many different seeds".
Held out
Matches deliberately kept away from the search, used only at the very end to check the winner honestly. Same idea as not letting students see the exam questions in advance.
Best-of-N bias
The reason a maximum over many noisy scores is always too optimistic. The whole point of section 3.
Step size
How widely scattered the current round of guesses is. Starts wide, shrinks as the search homes in.
Hall of fame
Grading a candidate against a field of opponents instead of one fixed opponent, so the winner has to be good in general rather than just good against whatever was in the Opponent box. Section 4.
Archive
The .json file a hall-of-fame run keeps its past winners in. Grows by one entry each time a run finishes; point every run at the same file to make the field harder to beat over time.
Exploitability
How much the single best counter in the field beat the winner by, on the held-out matches. 0 means it won every matchup in that field — not that it cannot lose.
Payoff matrix
The row-by-row breakdown behind exploitability: the winner's score against each opponent in the field, and that opponent's score back.
